Jev: what it is and where it fits

Short version

Jev makes decisions, it doesn't write. Text goes in, and a typed answer comes out: pick one, a score, or a yes/no probability, each with a confidence number. It answers in 70-500ms and costs $0.042 per million input tokens, with output free. It is worth using in three places: Zelta, Gulab and Naksha. The rest are skipped.

What it is

Jev is the first "System One" model from TypeSafe AI, founded by Diogo Almeida, who worked on the instruction-following research behind ChatGPT at OpenAI. You send a state (any text or JSON) and a set of questions. The model answers all of them in parallel, in a single call.

Question typeWhat you get back
ChoiceOne option from a list you define (up to 255), with a probability for each option
ScoreA level on an ordered scale you describe (e.g. calm / frustrated / angry), with a probability for each level
NoulThe probability that a yes/no statement is true

Limits verified: text only (no images, audio or video), 64k tokens per request, 1,200 requests per minute. TypeSafe says those limits "can change without notice" while demand is high. There is no fine-tuning. You shape its answers by what you put in the state and by how you word the questions.

Claims vs proof

ClaimStatus
Price $0.042 per million input tokens, output freeverified on their models page. They say themselves they can't prove it isn't subsidised.
70-500ms per answerTheir number. One independent tester on HN saw 100-200ms model time in the playground. Their servers are on the US West Coast, so calls from India add network time.
"193.6x faster, 444.6x cheaper" than big modelsunverified. The numbers come from their own workflow tests, written by their own team and scored against answers from other labs' models. TypeSafe calls them "the higher end of real world gains".
"Can't hallucinate"Only half true. It can't return an answer outside the options you defined. It can still pick the wrong option, as HN commenters point out and as TypeSafe's own list of weak spots shows.
Probabilities are calibrated (80% means right about 80% of the time)unverified for our own data. Test it on a labelled sample before trusting the thresholds.

Weak spots (TypeSafe's own list)

Keep these in code, not in Jev:

  • maths, counting and number comparisons
  • date comparisons
  • questions that need several reasoning steps
  • long inputs full of irrelevant detail
  • writing any text

It reads instructions literally. Text written to manipulate it can shift the answer, so user-written text needs tight criteria.

Adoption: Vercel added it to its AI gateway (free until Sep 25), and it is also listed on OpenRouter. An HN post quotes Vercel saying it was the fastest-adopted model on its gateway, used by about 13% of paid teams within 24 hours unverified (I couldn't find that at the original source).

The sceptics' case, from HN: sorting, scoring and routing are old problems; structured outputs from regular models already give valid formats; the launch wording oversold it; there is no obvious moat; and open copies already run on home hardware. All fair. The real gain is speed and cost at volume, not a new capability.

Access and data

  • Access: direct access is early access with a waitlist. It is also available through Vercel's gateway and OpenRouter, and there are Python and JavaScript SDKs.
  • Data: the privacy policy says they don't train on your input verified. Retention is "as long as reasonably necessary" and I found no terms specific to health data. Vercel's gateway offers a zero-retention setting. Nothing from a Zelta patient goes in until names and phone numbers are removed and the health-data terms are in writing.

Source: Privacy policy · Vercel changelog · Quick start

Where to start

  1. Zelta ad check. No patient data, can run this week, and it protects the account that brings in revenue.
  2. Gulab model router, in shadow mode. The direct cost cut.
  3. Zelta message triage, once the data terms are in writing.
  4. Naksha Ved routing after that.

Each one starts in shadow mode and gets compared with what people actually did, before it is allowed to act.
